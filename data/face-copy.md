# Outside the Line: speaker script

Reviewed 9 October 2026.

How to use it: say the "Say this" paragraph out loud while the slide is up, point at what is listed, and keep the rest for questions.

## 30-second version

New York started charging cars to enter lower Manhattan in January 2025, and a study found the air there about 22% cleaner than expected. But a community study in the South Bronx found small increases beside its expressways. We checked whether drivers detoured through the Bronx to dodge the fee: bridge counts and the city's Deegan counts say no. So the rise looks local, from trucks, idling or other sources near the highway, and nobody has proven the cause yet. Meanwhile we scored every stretch of street in the city on bad air, health and room for trees; East 138th Street in Mott Haven came out near the top. Our plan: survey six tree gaps there and plant now, at about $3,110 a tree, and later study a garden deck over the Deegan like Klyde Warren Park in Dallas, measuring the air before and after.

## Slide 1: A study estimated cleaner air in the paid zone. What happened in the South Bronx?

**Say this**

Congestion pricing started on January 5, 2025: drivers now pay to enter Manhattan at or below 60th Street. A study by Timothy Fraser and colleagues estimated that the zone's daily peak of fine particles, called PM2.5, was about 22% lower than their no-fee model predicted: 3.05 micrograms per cubic meter below a predicted 13.8. But a community study using South Bronx Unite's air sensors found small increases: +0.22 micrograms per cubic meter across its network, about 3% of the neighborhood's 2024 average of 6.89, and +1.29 at the worst sensor beside an expressway, about 19%. That 19% is an upper-end figure, because air beside a highway starts dirtier than the neighborhood average. So we asked three questions: did drivers dodge the fee by driving through the Bronx, what else could explain the rise, and what can the city do about it now and later?

**Point at**

- The Fraser chart: the paid zone's daily peak, 3.05 µg/m³ below the predicted 13.8 µg/m³ (about 22%).
- The air table: +0.22 µg/m³ across the South Bronx network (about 3%) and +1.29 µg/m³ at the worst sensor (about 19%).
- The Deegan photo: the highway runs right beside homes.

**If someone asks**

**Why are the 22% and the 19% shown separately?**

They come from different studies with different starting points. The 22% compares the zone's daily peak with a modeled no-fee level of 13.8 µg/m³; the 19% and 3% compare hourly step changes with the 2024 neighborhood average of 6.89 µg/m³. So we put them side by side for size, but we never subtract one from the other.

**What the units mean**

PM2.5 means airborne particles generally 2.5 micrometers or smaller, small enough to reach deep into the lungs. We measure their concentration in micrograms per cubic meter, written µg/m³. A microgram is one millionth of a gram; a cubic meter is a cube of air one meter on each side. Higher µg/m³ means more particle mass in the same volume of air. The 2.5 describes particle size; µg/m³ describes how much is in the air.

**Where was PM2.5 22% lower?**

The study is A first look into congestion pricing in the United States: PM2.5 impacts after six months of New York City cordon pricing, by Timothy Fraser and colleagues (npj Clean Air, 2025). Inside Manhattan's Congestion Relief Zone, at or below 60th Street, it estimated a 3.05 µg/m³ reduction from a modeled no-fee concentration of 13.8 µg/m³: 3.05 ÷ 13.8 × 100 ≈ 22%. This is lower than a prediction of what would have happened without pricing, not a simple 2024-to-2025 drop. The paper also analyzed the five boroughs and metro area; it wasn't limited to the zone.

**Which source covers the South Bronx?**

We use published results from South Bronx Unite's community-managed air sensor network, analyzed in Impacts of New York City's Congestion Pricing Policy on PM2.5 in the South Bronx: Evidence from a Community-Managed Air Sensor Network, led by Alexander De Jesus with Columbia, Brown and CU Boulder. Its public 2026 abstract compares hourly readings from 19 monitors across January 2024–December 2025. The models adjust for seasonality, temperature, humidity, wind and residential heating. The estimated network-wide step increase was +0.22 µg/m³; the largest highway-adjacent estimate was +1.29 µg/m³. The study was undergoing peer review when these results were released.

**Why does +1.29 matter?**

+1.29 means 1.29 more micrograms of particles in every cubic meter of air, not a 1.29% rise. It is about 5.9 times the network's estimated +0.22 µg/m³ increase, and its 95% uncertainty interval, +0.85 to +1.70 µg/m³, stays above zero, so the authors call it statistically significant. To compare it with the 22% drop, we turned it into a percentage, which needs a starting level. The study does not publish that sensor's own starting level, so we use the closest official one: NYC Health's Community Air Survey put the 2024 yearly average for Hunts Point–Mott Haven at 6.89 µg/m³. Against that, +1.29 is about a 19% increase (1.29 ÷ 6.89 × 100), and the network's +0.22 is about 3%. The sensor sits beside a highway, where the air usually starts dirtier than the neighborhood average, so its true percentage is probably below 19%. For scale, +1.29 is also about 14% of 9.0 µg/m³, the yearly limit the EPA set in 2024. We show them side by side but never subtract one from the other: the 22% compares the zone's daily peak with a modeled no-fee level of 13.8 µg/m³, while the 19% and 3% compare hourly step changes with the 2024 neighborhood average of 6.89 µg/m³. That makes +1.29 a local warning worth investigating, not proof that diverted traffic caused it.

**What we set out to answer**

- Did drivers dodge the fee by cutting through the Bronx?
- What could explain the South Bronx increases?
- What can the city do about it, now and later?

**Numbers and limits**

- Fraser et al. (2025), npj Clean Air 1:39, doi 10.1038/s44407-025-00037-2. Daily-maximum PM2.5, modeled no-fee comparison, first six months of 2025. The 22% applies to six monitors inside the zone; the full study includes 42 monitors across the metro area.
- De Jesus et al. (2026), public abstract hosted by South Bronx Unite. Generalized additive mixed models of hourly PM2.5, January 2024–December 2025; 19 of a 65-monitor network analyzed. Overall step estimate +0.22 µg/m³, 95% interval +0.12 to +0.32; maximum +1.29, interval +0.85 to +1.70. Four monitors had significant increases, two significant decreases, and 13 intervals crossed zero.
- These are transcribed published estimates. We did not obtain or recompute the community's hourly sensor dataset. The abstract and press release do not identify the maximum monitor's address or supply its reference concentration. We do not pin that value to East 138th Street.
- A percentage needs a stated denominator: concentration change ÷ the relevant reference concentration × 100. The 13.8 µg/m³ no-fee prediction belongs to Fraser's zone analysis and cannot be used as the South Bronx denominator. Instead we use the New York City Community Air Survey (NYCCAS) 2024 annual mean for Hunts Point–Mott Haven (UHF 107), 6.89 µg/m³, from NYC Health's Environment & Health Data Portal; its 2025 value is 6.80 µg/m³. A highway-side sensor likely starts higher, so 19% is an upper-end estimate. The EPA's 2024 annual PM2.5 standard is 9.0 µg/m³.

## Slide 2: Did drivers dodge the fee through the Bronx? The MTA bridge test found no clear increase.

**Say this**

First question: did drivers dodge the fee through the Bronx? The fee's own entry data can't tell us, because the Bronx is outside the paid zone, so its chart shows zero Bronx entries. That zero doesn't mean zero Bronx traffic. So we used MTA bridge counts and compared 2025 with 2024 at four Bronx crossings and at three bridges far from the zone, which the fee should barely affect. Every Bronx crossing grew by less than 2%; the Whitestone grew the most, 1.62%. Compared with the far bridges, the Bronx crossings came out about 1% lower, and the 95% range runs from 3.6% lower to 1.6% higher. Because that range includes zero, there's no clear increase. The city's own highway counts agree: the Deegan carried about 130,000 vehicles a day, down about 1% in 2025, with trucks down 0.29%. We found no sign of a detour through the Bronx.

**Point at**

- The fee-gate chart: named entry points by area (Brooklyn 4, Queens 2, New Jersey 2, East 60th St 1, West 60th St 1, FDR Drive 1, West Side Highway 1, Bronx 0). The Bronx is outside the paid zone, so it has no gates.
- The bridge dots: change from 2024 to 2025 at each crossing. Every Bronx crossing grew less than 2%; the Whitestone, +1.62%, grew the most.

**If someone asks**

The map gave us our first hypothesis: drivers might avoid entering the paid zone by choosing a route through the Bronx. We mapped ten facility records representing nine MTA bridges and tunnels, with the RFK Bridge's Bronx and Manhattan arms counted separately. These records count vehicles crossing each facility. They don't identify people's destinations or trace individual routes.

**Why does the gate chart show zero in the Bronx?**

The congestion fee is charged for entering Manhattan's zone at or below 60th Street. The gate dataset groups detections at the zone's entry points; it has no Bronx detection region because the Bronx is outside that boundary. Its 12 named detection groups aren't 12 individual toll gantries. A driver arriving from the Bronx can still be charged upon entering the Manhattan zone. Zero here means no Bronx category in this entry dataset, not zero Bronx traffic or zero bridge tolls.

**What changed in the annual bridge counts?**

We used MTA Bridges and Tunnels Hourly Crossings: Beginning 2019 to compare January 1–December 31, 2024 with the same dates in 2025. Bronx–Whitestone rose from 49,893,667 to 50,700,608 vehicle-crossing events: (50,700,608 − 49,893,667) ÷ 49,893,667 × 100 = +1.62%. RFK Bronx rose 0.13%, Throgs Neck 0.14% and Henry Hudson 0.28%. Small annual increases alone do not establish fee-related diversion; we need a comparison with other crossings.

The monthly test compared 2024 growth against the same months in 2023, then 2025 growth against the same months in 2024. Its adjusted relative-change estimate for the Bronx group was −1.04%, with a 95% range from −3.63% to +1.61%. That range includes zero, so the test found no clear increase relative to the comparison bridges. It cannot rule out a smaller increase or diversion onto roads outside this dataset.

**What the other traffic files could tell us**

MTA Congestion Relief Zone Vehicle Entries: Beginning 2025 described entry gates and traffic from January 5, 2025 through our September 5, 2026 pull, but supplied no 2024 baseline. NYC DOT's Automated Traffic Volume Counts supplied 15-minute road studies, but no usable matching before-and-after counts on our South Bronx roads. We also checked Vehicle Classification Counts (2011–2025) and Traffic Volume Counts (Historical); neither supported that local comparison. We have not yet used NYC DOT's Bridges & Crossings Traffic Volumes Dashboard, Port Authority monthly traffic reports or MTA's daily and older hourly files.

**Read the bottom graph**

- Bronx routes means four MTA crossing records: Bronx–Whitestone, Henry Hudson, Throgs Neck and the RFK Bronx arm. It is a group of crossings, not all traffic inside the borough.
- Comparison bridges means Cross Bay, Marine Parkway and Verrazzano–Narrows. We use them as a comparison, without assuming they were completely unaffected.
- Zone-serving tunnels means Hugh L. Carey and Queens Midtown. These are the two MTA tunnels in this test, not every tunnel entering Manhattan.
- All of 2025 combined means one adjusted model estimate across the group's crossings and all 12 months. The underlying unit is vehicle crossings per month; the plotted unit is percent difference in the year-over-year growth factor relative to the comparison bridges.
- A dot is the estimate; its line is a 95% uncertainty interval. Zero means no adjusted relative difference. The Bronx interval crosses zero, so there is no clear increase. The tunnel dot is lower, but a separate check for the small number of crossings gives p = 0.0625, so we don't treat it as definitive at the 5% threshold.

**The city's own Deegan count**

We also checked NYC Health's August 2026 report, Air Quality Impacts of Congestion Relief Zone Tolling, and its Detailed Traffic Analysis Methodology appendix. Table A-1 reports Major Deegan annual-average daily traffic of 129,955 vehicles/day in 2024 and 128,727 in 2025: (128,727 − 129,955) ÷ 129,955 × 100 = −0.94%. Table A-2 reports trucks declining from 10,326 to 10,296/day, or −0.29%. These are the agencies' seasonally adjusted estimates from periodic highway counts, not a rerun of our bridge model. They do not measure every street or explain a particular air sensor.

## Slide 3: Why can an air study find an increase when traffic counts do not?

**Say this**

So if traffic didn't go up, why did some sensors? First, they didn't rise everywhere: 4 of the 19 sensors rose clearly, and all four sit near expressways; 2 fell and 13 showed no clear change. Second, a traffic count tells you how many vehicles passed, not how much exhaust they made. More diesel trucks, more idling or more night trips can raise pollution without raising the count. In the paid zone, 31.8% of big-truck entries came overnight, versus 19.7% for cars; we don't have that split for the Deegan. Third, could it be global warming or the weather? The study already adjusted for weather, and air got cleaner elsewhere: Houston fell 7.1% and downtown Manhattan fell 13.1%. At city roadside monitors the fee's effect went both ways: the Queensboro improved 9.08 micrograms more than expected, while the Cross Bronx improved 3.74 less. So the cause looks local, right beside the highways, and it isn't proven yet.

**Point at**

- Top panel: downtown Manhattan 8.70 → 7.56 µg/m³, Houston 13.10 → 12.17 µg/m³, South Bronx network +0.22 µg/m³, worst sensor +1.29 µg/m³.
- Bottom panel, city roadside monitors against what was expected without the fee: Queensboro −9.08, Broadway/West 35th +2.46, Cross Bronx +3.74, Williamsburg +6.30 µg/m³.

**If someone asks**

**Short answer: why could the air get worse if traffic did not?**

First, it did not get worse everywhere. In the community study, 4 of 19 sensors showed a clear rise, and all four sit near expressways; 2 showed a clear drop and 13 showed no clear change. The city's own monitor study found no clear change tied to the fee at the environmental-justice sites it checked, and NYC Health's yearly air survey even puts the Hunts Point–Mott Haven average slightly lower in 2025 (6.80 µg/m³) than in 2024 (6.89 µg/m³). Second, a traffic count tells you how many vehicles passed, not how much exhaust they made. The same count can pollute more if more of the vehicles are diesel trucks, if traffic crawls or idles, or if trips move to night hours, when air stays low and mixes less. We have not tested that timing idea; it needs hourly counts. Third, other sources add particles too, such as construction, building heat, cooking and wildfire smoke. So nobody has proven the cause yet. The rest of this page shows what we checked.

**Traffic counts and air sensors measure different things**

The community study estimated an adjusted +0.22 µg/m³ change across 19 monitors, with clear increases at four sensors near expressways; the address of the sensor with the largest rise is unpublished. Our MTA test counted vehicles crossing bridges, not exhaust near those sensors, and NYC Health's highway tables show Deegan traffic down 0.94% and trucks down 0.29% from 2024 to 2025. The same number of vehicles can still make more pollution if the mix shifts toward diesel trucks, if traffic idles or crawls, or if speeds and routes change. Smoke, building heat, cooking, construction and the way air moves between buildings also change what a sensor reads. The community model adjusted for seasonality, temperature, humidity, wind and residential heating, which removes some of those effects but does not name the source of what is left. NYC Health's own monitors found no significant pricing-related change at the environmental-justice sites it checked, so the two air studies disagree; they use different monitor locations, sampling methods and statistical models. To settle it, we need calibrated air readings placed side by side with local vehicle types, speeds, idling and weather over the same dates.

**Could global warming explain the air-quality change?**

Global warming can affect air pollution through wildfire smoke and changing weather. To check whether worsening air also appeared elsewhere, we used EPA Air Quality System annual records for Houston North Wayside (site 48-201-0046). Annual PM2.5 fell from 13.10 to 12.17 µg/m³, a 7.1% decrease. Thomas J. Barber’s May 2026 Cornell thesis, The New York City Congestion Charge: Air Quality, Traffic, and Crime, reports CBD before/after means of 8.70 and 7.56 µg/m³ (Table A.8); its monthly air panel spans May 2024–June 2025. That is a 13.1% decrease from the rounded means, not a calendar-year comparison. In Houston, the fall is 0.93 µg/m³; 0.93 ÷ 13.10 × 100 ≈ 7.1%. One monitor in Houston cannot rule out global warming or New York's local weather. The 2025 Houston record is only 84% complete and is not certified.

**Why use the Van Wyck in Queens?**

The Van Wyck Expressway in Queens was the comparison site in NYC Health's August 2026 report, Air Quality Impacts of Congestion Relief Zone Tolling. A comparison site is a place the fee should barely affect, so its readings show what weather and citywide trends did on their own. The city used the Van Wyck readings, plus weather and time trends, to predict what each other site would have measured without the fee. The gap between that prediction and the real reading is the part that could come from pricing. At the environmental-justice sites it evaluated, the city found no clear pricing-related PM2.5 change. This is a different study from the Queensboro chart.

**How the bottom graph was calculated**

The bottom graph transcribes Goldberg and colleagues' preprint, Variable Short-Term Air Quality Impacts of New York City's Congestion Pricing Policy. It compares each site's July–January versus January–June change in 2024–25 with the corresponding change in 2023–24. The difference between those two changes is the plotted estimate: Queensboro −9.08 µg/m³, Williamsburg +6.30 µg/m³, Cross Bronx +3.74 µg/m³ and Broadway / West 35th +2.46 µg/m³. Those are adjusted differences, not raw annual changes or percentages. All six sites' raw January–June averages fell; a positive adjusted estimate means less improvement than the model expected. Two sites had no clear adjusted change and are omitted from this four-site panel.

**Where did 5.9 times come from?**

The largest South Bronx estimate, +1.29 µg/m³, is 5.9 times the network estimate of +0.22. That compares changes; it does not say pollution was six times higher or that neighboring blocks were measured. The public abstract does not identify the maximum monitor's address. Separately, 31.8% of multi-unit truck entries into the paid zone were overnight, versus 19.7% of car entries. Those zone-entry counts cannot establish truck traffic on the Deegan.

## Slide 4: Why East 138th? How we scored every stretch of street, then ranked them.

**Say this**

Then we asked where the city should act. We joined 99,357 street segments into 33,785 overlapping stretches, each 0.8 to 2 kilometers long, and scored every one on eight factors in three groups: is the air likely bad, are people already sick or struggling, and will trees work there. Each group gets about a third of the score. Then we weighted by how many people live within 400 meters. East 138th Street in Mott Haven has about 25,910 residents per kilometer and a need score of 0.8725 out of 1. With every factor counted equally it ranked #22 of 33,785; with our weights it ranked #4 of the 35 finalist streets, and it stayed in the top five in 92.4% of 10,000 random weight mixes. The surrounding area's median income is $15,510, and 29.3% of people live in poverty. One caution: the air factor uses a monitor location that hasn't been verified.

**Point at**

- The weights chart: colored bars are our weights; gray bars are equal weights, 12.5% each.
- The rank chart: East 138th is #4 of the 35 finalist streets.
- The income map: the highlighted area's median income is $15,510 and 29.3% of people live in poverty.

**If someone asks**

**1. What did we compare, and when?**

In September 2026 we joined 99,357 NYC Street Centerline road segments into 33,785 overlapping candidate corridor windows. Each window is 0.8–2.0 km long, targeting 1.6 km. These are analysis units, not 33,785 different streets or blocks. The scan used historical sources: 2015 Street Tree Census, 2020 Census population, the NFH dataset marked published 2020, and 2023 asthma rates. They describe different years.

**2. Why choose these factors?**

Our aim is to identify places where residents face environmental burdens and where planting may be feasible. Exposure proxies address possible sources; asthma, financial vulnerability and heat address the people affected; tree gaps and street shape address whether planting is appropriate. These choices express our planning priorities. They are not scientifically fitted causal weights.

**3. Why the percentages are split this way**

Every stretch of street gets a score out of 100 points, split across three questions: Is the air likely bad? (33 points) Are people already sick or struggling? (33 points) Will trees work here? (34 points) The three get about equal say because a good site needs all three: bad air with no room for trees can't be planted, and room for trees where few people breathe the exhaust helps no one. 33 + 33 + 34 = 100, and the planting group's 34 splits evenly, 17 for the tree gap and 17 for street shape, because a site needs both. Inside each question, the most direct factor gets the most points and factors that overlap get fewer. Road distance gets 15 because every street has one. The air-monitor signal gets 10 because it only reaches streets within 1,500 m of a monitor and the South Bronx monitor's location is unverified. Highway adjacency gets 8 because it partly repeats road distance. Asthma gets 20 because breathing harm is the heart of the question; money gets 8 and heat gets 5, because the heat index already includes income-type factors. These are judgment calls, not fitted from data, so we tested them: with all eight factors equal at 12.5% each (the gray bars), East 138th ranked #22 of 33,785, and across 10,000 random weight mixes it stayed in the top five of the 35 finalists 92.4% of the time. The percentages are shares of the score, not pollution removed or people helped.

- 15% · Road proximity: distances in meters to bridges, ramps and highways, converted to a distance-decay proxy. Nearer roads raise the grade; this is not a traffic count.
- 10% · Published air-monitor signal: the sign of a published PM2.5 change, weakened with distance up to 1,500 m. A monitor is an instrument measuring air at one location, not an instrument on every listed street. The South Bronx monitor's exact location is not published, so this is the least certain factor.
- 8% · Highway adjacency: share of corridor length within 400 m of a raised or below-grade highway, from 0 to 1. This flags infrastructure exposure but partly overlaps with road proximity.
- 20% · Asthma: adult and child emergency-department visit rates per 10,000 residents, from NYC Health neighborhood data. This measures existing health burden, not cases caused by congestion pricing.
- 8% · Financial vulnerability: NFH median income in US dollars, poverty as a percentage, and financial-health rank by PUMA. A PUMA is a large Census statistical area. We use these to prioritize limited public investment, not to claim each resident has that income.
- 5% · Heat: NYC Heat Vulnerability Index, a score from 1 to 5 by ZIP-code area. It is vulnerability, not degrees of temperature, and already includes overlapping social factors.
- 17% · Tree gap: living street trees per 100 m in the 2015 census. Fewer street trees raise the grade; park trees and planting changes since 2015 can be missed.
- 17% · Street shape: building height divided by facade-to-facade street width, both measured in meters. The ratio is dimensionless. More open streets raise the suitability grade; elevated trains and site obstacles are not fully modeled.

**4. How do those factors become “need”?**

We orient each factor so a larger grade means a stronger planting case and usually convert it to a citywide percentile grade between 0 and 1; highway adjacency is already a length share. In the finalist score, a missing grade is assumed to be 0.5. Need = sum(weight × grade), so need is a dimensionless index from 0 to 1. A value of 0.8725 does not mean 87.25% pollution, poverty or illness. Missing-as-middle is an assumption, not evidence that the missing data are average.

**5. Then count residents; only then show the ranking**

Using 2020 Census block population, we count residents whose block center lies within 400 m of each corridor, then divide by corridor length in km. Priority = need × residents per km. This estimates residents near a planting corridor weighted by the index; it does not estimate people cured or benefits per dollar. For the selected 1.249 km East 138th window: 32,362 residents ÷ 1.249 km ≈ 25,910 residents/km. Its need score is 0.8725; 0.8725 × 25,910 ≈ 22,606 index-weighted residents/km.

**6. Why East 138th, and how sure are we?**

East 138th was #22 of 33,785 overlapping windows when every factor counted equally (12.5% each). With the weights above we re-scored the 47 best windows, covering 35 different streets: East 138th is #3 on need and #4 on need × residents/km; Jerome Avenue is #1 on the latter. Those are different rankings with different denominators. We chose East 138th as a corridor to investigate because it combines residential exposure, high neighborhood vulnerability and specific planting gaps, not because it is proven to have the worst air.

Across 10,000 random weight mixes among those same 35 finalists, East 138th remained in the top five on the population-weighted index in 92.4% of runs. That tests sensitivity to weights, not whether the source data are true. It does not fix the unlocated South Bronx monitor, stale tree census, block-center population approximation, neighborhood-level health data or streets excluded from the finalists. So treat these ranks as a guide to where to look first, not a final answer, until the monitor location is confirmed and the citywide scan is rerun.

**7. Why add the economic map?**

NYC DCWP’s Neighborhood Financial Health dataset (r3dx-pew9) reports median income of $15,510 and poverty of 29.3% for PUMA 3710, Hunts Point, Longwood and Melrose. Its $31,238 comparison is the unweighted average of 55 PUMA median incomes, not NYC’s household median. Four nearby CDBG tracts contain 14,525 residents; that is a separate geography from the 32,362 Census residents within the East 138th window’s 400 m buffer. The map shows where these neighborhood indicators apply and why financial vulnerability enters the score.

**What could missing data change?**

A quiet road near a highway could score high despite low traffic. A neighborhood asthma average could hide a healthier block. Missing local monitors could hide either an increase or a decrease. A 2015 tree count may miss trees planted or removed since then. Wrong monitor coordinates can change the air proxy and ranking. Before selecting an investment, verify sensor locations, recount trees, obtain matched street traffic counts and check utilities, access and planting space on site.

**Why the zoning map cannot choose the site**

FAR means floor area ratio: building floor area divided by lot area. A FAR of 2 permits floor area equal to twice the lot area, subject to other zoning rules. A wide low building and a narrow taller building can have the same FAR. Yellow is not inherently a better garden site than red. This historical zoning image provides development context; it does not measure sidewalk width, tree pits, airflow or whether a highway deck can be built. We choose inspection locations from 2015 counts and measured site conditions, not FAR colors.

We are confident that the 2015 counts are uneven; we are less certain they describe today’s planting space. **How 10.6 pounds per year was calculated**

PM2.5 is fine airborne particulate matter. Here, pounds/year measures modeled particle mass removed; µg/m³ measures concentration in air. The complete scenario assumes 180 mature 7 m tree crowns, 40,000 ft² of green wall and 8 acres of green roof. ## How sure are we?

We can confidently identify the source, unit and calculation of each reported result. Confidence in a causal explanation or a final site is lower. The South Bronx abstract, Houston annual series, city roadside preprint and bridge comparison measure different things. Their disagreement can reflect coverage, timing and methods; it cannot be resolved by averaging their numbers.

- **Local traffic is missing.** A detour on the Deegan or a free bridge could occur without appearing in the MTA crossing test. Obtain matched local counts and comparable control roads.

- **The peak sensor's address and starting level are unpublished.** We cannot place +1.29 µg/m³ on East 138th. Our 19% uses the 6.89 µg/m³ neighborhood average, not the sensor's own starting level, so it is an upper-end estimate. Obtain the coordinates and the baseline, then rerun the street scan.

- **Houston coverage is incomplete.** 2025 has 307 valid days of 365 and is not certified. If missing days differ from observed days, its annual mean can be biased. One distant monitor cannot rule out New York weather or global warming.

- **Street inputs differ in year and scale.** Trees are from 2015, population from 2020 and asthma rates from 2023 neighborhood areas. Updated counts and corrected geography joins can change a shortlist. A neighborhood average cannot describe every block or person.

- **Missing grades were assigned 0.5.** That can hide unusually high or low burdens. The 10,000 weight simulations measure weight sensitivity among 35 finalists, not data accuracy or certainty of being best citywide.

- **The deck is a dimensioned concept.** Unknown grades, ramps, utilities, foundations and exhaust portals can make the shape unbuildable or worsen exposure. A survey, engineering design and dispersion assessment must precede a construction claim.

A reported 95% model interval reflects uncertainty under its model assumptions. It is not a 95% probability that the story is true, and it does not account for every missing dataset or systematic error. We report “no clear increase” when the interval crosses zero. We give no invented numerical confidence for the planting site or deck.
